The Ageless walk-through

What a home safety assessment actually covers.

This is the framework we use in your home — a structured evaluation conducted alongside you and, if you wish, your family. It moves from conversation, to a room-by-room inspection, to a shared plan the household can act on.

Book an assessment

This is an assessment of the home and how well it supports the way you live — not a test of the person, and not a medical evaluation. You are the decision-maker throughout. Our role is to observe carefully, explain what we see, and lay out options. Families are welcome to print this page to follow along during the visit.

Working principles

How the assessor works

  • Lead with goals, not deficits. We open by asking what you want to keep doing independently. Every finding is framed against that goal — “this makes the morning routine harder than it needs to be,” not “you can’t manage this.”
  • Ask before entering or opening anything. Rooms, closets, cabinets, drawers — permission every time, with a plain explanation of what we’re checking and why.
  • Demonstration at your pace. We may ask you to show how you get in and out of the tub, rise from a chair, or take the stairs. We watch; we don’t coach mid-task unless safety requires it.
  • Measurements, not impressions. Step rise, door width, grab-bar height, seat height, water temperature. Numbers keep the report objective.
  • “Fail” is a property condition, not a judgment. It marks something worth discussing. Whether anything changes is your call, and the plan says so plainly.
  • Family input is welcome; your voice is final. If you and a family member disagree, the plan records both views rather than resolving it for you.
  • Language we keep out of the room: “elderly,” “still able to,” “for someone your age,” “we just need to….” We talk about the house, the task, and the choices.

Part 1 · Pre-assessment questions

Asked seated, before walking the home, with no rush. Groups B, D, and H usually open up the most useful information.

A · The home & living situation

  • How long have you lived here, and do you plan to stay for the foreseeable future?
  • Which rooms and floors do you use on a normal day? Are there rooms you’ve stopped using — and what made you stop?
  • Which entrance do you normally use? Any you avoid, and why?
  • Do you use stairs every day? Is there a step-free way in and out?
  • Who else lives here, including pets? Roughly what size are the pets, and where are they underfoot?
  • Do you own, rent, or live in a family-owned home? This tells us whether permanent changes — drilling grab bars, altering a handrail — are on the table, or whether we design around freestanding options.
  • Where are the laundry, the heating and cooling controls, the electrical panel, and the water shut-off? Do you reach any of them by stairs?

B · Daily routines & what independence means to you

  • Walk me through a normal morning, from waking up to being ready for the day.
  • Which activities matter most to you to keep doing on your own?
  • What’s become harder over the past year that you’d like to make easier again?
  • What does a good day at home look like? What would put that at risk?
  • Is there anything you’d like to do here that the house currently makes difficult?
  • If we changed only one or two things today, what would you most want them to be?

C · Getting around

  • Do you use a cane, walker, wheelchair, or hold onto furniture as you move around? Indoors, outdoors, or both?
  • Any changes in balance, dizziness, or light-headedness — on standing, at night, in the shower, or when you first wake?
  • How are stairs for you right now — going up compared with coming down?
  • Can you get up from a standard chair, the toilet, and the bed without pushing off hard or needing a hand?
  • How far can you walk comfortably before resting? Any pain or weakness that affects walking?
  • Do you feel steady carrying something in both hands — laundry, groceries, a full plate?

D · Falls & near-misses

  • In the past 12 months, have you slipped, tripped, lost your balance, or fallen? How many times?
  • For each fall: where were you, what were you doing, what time was it, were you hurt, and could you get back up on your own?
  • Any near-misses where you caught yourself? Where do those tend to happen?
  • If you fell and couldn’t get up, what’s the plan? Could you call for help from the floor?
  • Do you ever move around less, or skip something, because you’re concerned about falling?

Why we ask: a prior fall is the single strongest predictor of the next one. The details tell us which zone to prioritise.

E · Vision, hearing & daytime alertness

  • When was your last eye exam? Do you wear glasses — single-vision, or bifocals / progressives? Progressive lenses blur the feet and the stair edge when you look down.
  • Any trouble with glare, dim light, or how long your eyes take to adjust between a bright and a dark room?
  • Any hearing changes that would make a smoke alarm, a knock, or the phone hard to hear — especially from the bedroom?
  • Do you get up during the night? How many times, and how is the lighting on that route?
  • Do any of your medications leave you drowsy, unsteady, or light-headed — and at what time of day?

F · Running the household

  • What do you cook, and how often? Any burns, cuts, a forgotten burner, or a scare in the kitchen?
  • Do you reach overhead or bend low for everyday items? Do you ever stand on a stool or a chair to reach something?
  • Where’s the laundry, and does getting to it involve stairs or carrying a full basket?
  • How do you handle light bulbs, smoke-alarm batteries, the thermostat, and small repairs?
  • Do you shower, bathe, or both? Walk me through getting in and out. Is any part of it unsteady?

G · Support & emergencies

  • Who do you call first if you need help quickly, and how soon could they be here?
  • Do you keep a phone on you during the day? Overnight?
  • Do you have a medical-alert device or a watch with fall detection? Do you wear it consistently — and if not, what gets in the way?
  • Are the smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms working? When were they last tested?
  • Is there a clear way out of the home in an emergency, including in the dark?
  • Does a trusted neighbour or family member have a key or lockbox access?

H · What’s already in place

  • What safety equipment do you already have — bars, a shower chair, a raised seat, a reacher? Is it working for you?
  • What changes have you already made to the home? What helped, and what didn’t?
  • Has a physical or occupational therapist, or home-health nurse, made recommendations here before? Were any carried out?
  • Has your family suggested anything you don’t agree with? Better to hear it now and design a plan everyone can live with.

Part 2 · Room-by-room checklist

During the visit, each line is marked Pass, Fail, or N/A. “Pass” means the condition already supports safe, independent use. “Fail” means it’s worth discussing and likely belongs in the action plan. The Equip note names a low-cost, high-impact option. Items that fail in most homes are marked Frequently fails.

  • Cost key:
  • $ under $25
  • $$ $25–75
  • $$$ $75–200
  • $$$$ over $200 or professional install

Zone 1 · Exterior approach & entry

  • Path from the street or driveway to the door is even, firm, and free of cracks, frost-heave, moss, or loose gravel.Looks like: no lip over ¼″; no rocking slabs; grips underfoot when damp.
  • Path is wide enough for a walker or wheelchair, now or if one is anticipated.Looks like: clear width ≥ 36″.
  • Entry steps have a graspable handrail on at least one side — both sides preferred — that stays firm under full weight. Frequently failsLooks like: rail 34–38″ above the step nosing; no wobble.Equip: add an aluminium or steel handrail ($$$ + install); a foam grip sleeve ($) is an interim fix for an ungrippable rail.
  • Entry steps are uniform in height, sound, and have visible edges.Equip: high-contrast non-slip tread strips or photoluminescent edge tape, top and bottom step first ($).
  • Landing at the door is level and deep enough to stand safely while managing keys and the door.Looks like: ≥ 36″ deep; ideally 60″ × 60″ if a mobility device is used.
  • Threshold at the entry door is low or bevelled — no raised strip to catch a toe or a wheel.Looks like: ≤ ½″, or ramped.Equip: rubber or aluminium threshold ramp / wedge ($$–$$$).
  • You can operate the entry lock easily.Equip: lever handle in place of a knob ($–$$); keypad or keyless deadbolt ($$–$$$).
  • Exterior lighting covers the path, the steps, and the lock, and switches from inside and out. Frequently failsEquip: motion-activated LED porch and path lights, solar or wired ($–$$ each).
  • Collecting mail or parcels doesn’t require taking steps without a free hand for the rail.
  • House number is visible from the street, day and night, for emergency responders.Equip: reflective or illuminated house numbers ($–$$).

Zone 2 · Stairways & steps (interior and exterior)

  • A continuous handrail runs the full length of every stair flight, on at least one side; both sides preferred where descending is harder.Looks like: no gaps at the newel post; rail present for every step of the run.Equip: second handrail ($$$ + install); the descending side matters most.
  • The handrail is easy to wrap a hand around and returns toward the wall at each end so a sleeve or bag can’t snag.Looks like: round profile roughly 1¼–2″ across; 34–38″ above the nosings; 1½″ clearance to the wall.
  • The handrail extends horizontally about 12″ beyond the top and bottom steps. Frequently failsLooks like: something to hold before the first step down and after the last step up.Equip: bolt-on stair-rail extension / return fittings ($–$$ per end; install $$).
  • The rail is anchored into framing and doesn’t move when leaned on hard.Equip: re-anchor into studs or add blocking ($$ handyperson).
  • Every tread is the same depth and every riser the same height — no single odd step.Looks like: variation under 3/8″ across the flight; flag any “surprise” step for a contrast marker.
  • Tread surfaces are non-slip; any carpet is tight and unworn; no loose runner, mat, or rug at the top or bottom.Equip: non-slip stair tread strips ($); re-secure or remove a loose runner.
  • Step edges are easy to see — a contrasting strip on the nosing, at minimum on the top and bottom steps. Frequently failsEquip: high-contrast anti-slip nosing tape ($); photoluminescent for night visibility.
  • The stairway is well lit, with switches at both the top and the bottom, and no glare or deep shadow across the treads.Equip: add a three-way switch ($$ electrician); plug-in or rechargeable motion stair lights as an interim ($–$$).
  • Nothing is stored or left on the stairs; no low pet gate that itself becomes a trip hazard.
  • Landings top and bottom are level, clear, and not opening straight into a door swing.
  • If stairs are a daily struggle: you’re open to discussing a stair lift, a ramp, or moving daily activities to one level.Note: raised as an option to weigh, on your timeline — not a recommendation to accept today.

Zone 3 · Hallways & transitions

  • Hallways are clear of clutter, low furniture, and cords; a walker can pass.Looks like: ≥ 36″ clear, ≥ 32″ at the tightest pinch point.
  • Flooring is consistent and transitions between rooms or surfaces are flush or bevelled.Looks like: height change ≤ ½″; no metal transition strip standing proud.
  • Throw rugs are removed, or secured on all edges and lying flat. Frequently failsEquip: double-sided rug tape or a non-slip pad ($); removal is the safer fix.
  • No electrical or phone cords cross a walking path, and none run under a rug.Equip: cord covers / raceways along the baseboard ($); add an outlet nearer the point of use ($$).
  • Lighting is even along the whole route with no dark stretch, and switches sit at both ends and by room doorways.Equip: higher-output bulbs; stick-on switch-plate LEDs; three-way switch ($$).
  • There is a lit path from the bedroom to the bathroom for night use. Frequently failsEquip: plug-in motion night lights at ankle height, one every 6–8 ft ($ each).
  • Along any long wall where balance is a concern, there’s a deliberate handhold — a rail, or sturdy furniture placed for the purpose.Equip: stud-mounted wall rail ($$ + install).
  • Doors don’t swing unexpectedly into the path; problem doors can be removed, reversed, or held back.

Zone 4 · Bathroom — primary focus

Wet, hard, and enclosed — the highest-consequence room in most homes. Completed for every bathroom you use, with a demonstration of the transfer into and out of the tub or shower.

General

  • The door can be unlocked from the outside, and ideally swings outward or slides, so help can reach someone who has fallen against it. Frequently failsEquip: privacy lever set with an emergency exterior release ($–$$); re-hang the door to swing out ($$$).
  • The floor is not slippery when wet; any bath mat has a full non-slip backing and lies flat.Equip: low-pile mat with rubber backing ($); non-slip floor treatment for tile ($–$$).
  • Lighting is bright and even at the mirror and inside the shower or tub, with a night light for overnight trips.Equip: higher-output bulbs; a damp-rated shower light ($$); plug-in night light ($).

Toilet

  • You can sit and stand without pulling on the sink, the towel bar, or the tank.
  • Seat height suits you.Looks like: 17–19″ to the top of the seat; feet flat, knees roughly level with hips.Equip: raised toilet seat ($–$$); comfort-height toilet ($$$$); toilet-seat riser with arms ($$).
  • A dedicated grab bar or a floor-mounted toilet safety frame is within reach — the towel bar is not load-bearing. Frequently failsEquip: floor-mounted safety frame, no drilling ($$); wall grab bar into studs ($–$$ + install $$); tank-mounted rail ($) for lighter support.
  • Toilet paper is reachable without twisting or leaning far off the seat.

Tub & shower

  • A properly mounted grab bar is at the entry point — a vertical bar at the threshold gives a hold for the step in and out. Frequently failsLooks like: 1¼–1½″ diameter · 1½″ wall clearance · anchored to studs or rated heavy-duty anchors · rated ≥ 250 lb · never a suction-cup bar as a load-bearing hold.Equip: vertical grab bar at the threshold ($–$$ + install $$).
  • A horizontal grab bar runs along the long wall inside for balance while standing and washing; a second bar is added where needed.Equip: 24–36″ horizontal bar ($–$$ each + install $$).
  • The tub or shower floor is non-slip.Equip: adhesive treads or a full-contact suction mat ($); non-slip coating ($$).
  • If stepping over the tub wall is hard, there’s a way to sit through the transfer instead.Equip: transfer bench or bath board spanning the wall ($$–$$$); longer term, a tub cut-out or walk-in shower ($$$$).
  • A shower chair or bench is available if wanted — rubber tips, adjustable height, drains well; back and arm rests if transfers are effortful.Equip: shower chair ($$) or bench with back and arms ($$–$$$).
  • A handheld shower head on a slide bar lets you wash while seated and rinse without standing or turning. Frequently failsEquip: handheld shower with a 5–6 ft hose and slide bar ($–$$; no plumber needed).
  • Water temperature is safe and controllable from a seated position; the heater is set to a safe maximum.Looks like: water heater ≤ 120°F; single-lever or thermostatic valve; controls reachable without standing.Equip: anti-scald / thermostatic valve ($$–$$$); lever handle retrofit ($–$$).
  • Soap, shampoo, and a washcloth are within reach from where you sit or stand to wash — no bending to the floor or reaching overhead.Equip: suction or bar-mounted corner caddy at mid-height ($).
  • The shower curtain or door opens without a hard pull, and no curtain rod or towel bar is positioned where it might be grabbed for support.

Sink & vanity

  • You can reach and turn the faucet without leaning over a hot surface or losing balance.Equip: lever or touch faucet ($–$$).
  • Everyday items — toothbrush, medications, razor — are in a reachable drawer or shelf, not only a high cabinet.
  • You don’t rely on the sink or vanity as a push-up support — check for movement, cracked caulk, loose mounting bolts.

Zone 5 · Kitchen

  • Everyday dishes, food, and cookware sit between knee and shoulder height — nothing used weekly needs a step stool or a deep bend. Frequently failsEquip: pull-down wall-cabinet shelf ($$$); lazy-Susans and drawer organisers to bring items forward ($); the best fix is often just rearranging.
  • If reaching high is unavoidable, there is a stable step stool with a high hand-hold — never a chair.Equip: step stool with a support handle ($$); reacher / grabber for light high items ($).
  • The floor is free of slip hazards — no loose mat at the sink, spills show against the floor colour, and there’s a wipe-it-now habit.Equip: anti-fatigue mat with a bevelled, non-slip edge at the sink ($–$$).
  • There is landing counter space beside the stove and beside the fridge, and a clear run to slide heavy pots rather than carry them.Equip: rolling kitchen cart to move heavy or hot items and to steady a load ($$).
  • Stove controls are at the front, so there’s no reaching across a hot burner, and the markings are legible.Equip: large-print control overlays ($); a stove shut-off timer or a smart plug for small appliances ($–$$).
  • A working smoke alarm covers the kitchen, sited to avoid nuisance trips, and a fire extinguisher you can lift and operate is within reach.Equip: photoelectric alarm 10+ ft from the range ($–$$); 2–5 lb extinguisher ($$).
  • Task lighting reaches the stove, the sink, and the main counter, with no shadow on the work surface.Equip: stick-on or plug-in under-cabinet LED strips ($–$$).
  • Sharp knives are stored in a block or guarded, not loose in a drawer.
  • Cleaning chemicals are stored away from food and not overhead, with legible labels.
  • A phone or call device is reachable from the kitchen in case of a burn or a fall.

Zone 6 · Bedroom

  • A light can be switched on from the bed without getting up, and there’s a lit path to the door and the bathroom. Frequently failsEquip: touch lamp or bedside remote outlet switch ($); plug-in motion night lights on the route ($).
  • Bed height lets you sit with both feet flat and stand without dropping down or climbing up.Looks like: roughly 20–23″ to the top of the mattress.Equip: bed risers to raise ($), or swap the frame / remove risers to lower.
  • There’s a firm edge or handhold to push up from on the exit side, if transfers are hard.Equip: bed rail or bed cane with a stable base, or a floor-to-ceiling pole ($$–$$$) — fit the correct type and size to avoid an entrapment gap.
  • The floor beside the bed is clear — no cords, small rug, slippers, or pet bed in the step-out zone.
  • Phone and, if used, the medical-alert device are within reach of the bed at night.Equip: bedside caddy for phone, glasses, and alert device ($).
  • Clothing worn daily is between knee and shoulder height — no deep bend into a low drawer or reach to a high closet shelf.Equip: closet rod lowered or a pull-down rod ($$); reacher ($).
  • A sturdy chair with arms is in the room for dressing while seated.
  • If the only bedroom is up a flight you find hard, you’re open to discussing a main-floor sleeping option.Note: presented as an option, on your timeline.

Zone 7 · Living areas & whole-home basics

  • Your main chair or sofa lets you rise easily — firm seat, sturdy arms running the full seat depth.Looks like: seat height 17–19″; you rise in one motion without rocking.Equip: chair risers ($) or a lift-assist seat cushion ($$–$$$); replace a low, soft chair if it can’t be raised safely.
  • Walking paths through the room are at least 36″ and reasonably straight — coffee tables and ottomans out of the main line.
  • All rugs are secured or removed; no rug laid over carpet; runner ends tacked down. Frequently failsEquip: rug tape or non-slip pad ($); removal preferred.
  • Cords run along walls, never across a path or under a rug, and outlets aren’t overloaded.Equip: cord raceways ($); an added outlet to remove an extension cord ($$).
  • Lighting reaches every seating and walking area; switches are reachable on entry; bare bulbs and window glare are controlled.Equip: three-way or touch lamps ($–$$); higher-output bulbs; a plug-in switch by the door ($).
  • The thermostat is readable and reachable, and there’s no reliance on a portable heater sitting in a walkway.Equip: large-display or voice thermostat ($$–$$$).
  • Everyday items — remote, phone, glasses, reading material — have a reachable home beside the main chair.Equip: armrest caddy or a small side table ($).
  • Windows you open for air or as a fire exit are reachable and not painted shut.
  • Pet feeding stations and beds are out of the walking paths.Equip: a bell on a cat or small dog’s collar ($).

Zone 8 · Alarms, communication & emergency egress

  • Smoke alarms are on every level and outside each sleeping area, interconnected if possible, with sealed 10-year batteries or tested monthly. Frequently failsEquip: 10-year sealed photoelectric alarms ($–$$ each); wireless-interconnect set ($$$).
  • A carbon-monoxide alarm is near the sleeping areas if there is gas, oil, a fireplace, or an attached garage.Equip: plug-in or 10-year CO alarm ($$).
  • Alarms are loud enough to wake you from the bedroom with the door shut; a strobe and bed-shaker unit if hearing is a concern.Equip: strobe / bed-shaker alarm ($$–$$$).
  • There are at least two ways out, and you can unlock and open exit doors quickly — including deadbolts and any security bars.Looks like: keys for a double-cylinder deadbolt kept in the lock or immediately beside it.
  • Exit routes are clear and can be walked in the dark.Equip: photoluminescent path marking; motion egress lighting ($–$$).
  • A phone is reachable from floor level in the likely fall spots — bathroom, bedroom, kitchen, main chair. Frequently failsEquip: medical-alert pendant or fall-detection smartwatch ($$–$$$ + monthly), worn consistently; cordless handsets on low tables.
  • Emergency contacts are posted where a responder would see them, and a trusted neighbour or family member has key or lockbox access.Equip: exterior lockbox ($).
  • A current medication list and key health information are somewhere EMS would find them — commonly on the fridge.

Part 3 · Priority action plan

A shared summary for the household. Every item is offered for your consideration; you decide what happens in your home. Low-cost items that one handyperson visit could resolve are grouped together.

P1 · Now Tied to a high fall or injury risk, or to a fall or near-miss already experienced. Usually low-cost and quick — aim to complete within a week.
P2 · ~30 days Meaningful risk reduction that needs a purchase, a tradesperson, or some scheduling.
P3 · Plan ahead Supports independence over the longer term — larger projects, or lower immediate urgency. Revisited at follow-up.

The plan also records

  • Your own words on what independence at home looks like.
  • Any findings you’ve chosen not to act on at this time.
  • A target completion date for P1 items and who is coordinating the work.
  • A re-assessment date — sooner after any fall, hospital stay, or change in mobility or vision.

This is an assessment of the home environment and how it supports your daily activities. It is not a medical or diagnostic evaluation. Where balance, strength, vision, cognition, or a recent fall suggest a clinical need, we recommend follow-up with your physician or a physical or occupational therapist. Cost bands are planning estimates only and vary by region, brand, and installation. Every recommendation is offered for your consideration; decisions about what is installed or changed rest with you.
