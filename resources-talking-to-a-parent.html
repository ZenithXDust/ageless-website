How to talk to a parent about safety without making them defensive

The conversation usually goes badly for a predictable reason. Here is what is actually happening, and a different way to open it.

Why it goes wrong

You are having a conversation about safety. They are having a conversation about whether they are still a competent adult and whether this is the beginning of losing the house.

Those are not the same conversation, and until you notice you are in different ones, nothing you say will land. Every practical suggestion you make is heard as evidence in a case you did not realise you were prosecuting.

What they are actually afraid of

Rarely the sensor. Usually one of these:

  • That agreeing to one thing starts a slope towards a home.
  • That being watched is the price of staying.
  • That their judgement is now something to be managed by their children.
  • That saying yes is admitting something they are not ready to admit.

None of those are answered by explaining how good the technology is. They are answered by being explicit about what is not being proposed.

Five things that help

Lead with the goal, not the risk

"I want you to be able to stay here for as long as you want to" is true, is what everybody in the room actually wants, and puts you on the same side of the table. It is a very different opening from "I am worried about you."

Say out loud what you are not asking for

"No cameras. Nobody watching you. Nothing that reports on your day." If that is true, say it early, because they are assuming the opposite and will not ask.

Make it about you

"This would help me stop worrying" is easier to accept than "you need this." It is often also the more honest statement. A great deal of home safety equipment is bought to reduce a family's anxiety, and there is nothing wrong with saying so.

Give them the decision, genuinely

Offer two or three options and let them choose, including the option of doing nothing for now. A decision made under pressure gets reversed the moment you leave. A decision they actually made tends to stick.

Start with the least intrusive thing

Better lighting on the way to the bathroom. A kettle that switches itself off. Nobody has ever felt insulted by a lightbulb. Small changes that visibly help build the case for the larger ones far better than any argument will.

If it still goes badly

Leave it. A refusal today is not permanent, and pushing turns a temporary no into a position they now have to defend. Come back to it after something changes, and in the meantime do the things that do not need permission, like fixing the lighting.

It is also worth knowing that these conversations frequently go better when the family is not in the room. An outside person asking the same questions is not a son or a daughter implying anything, and people will often say things to a stranger that they will not say to their children.
