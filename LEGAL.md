# Legal documents: what they are and what they are not

`terms.html` and `privacy.html` are complete, thorough documents written to
cover the real exposure this business has. They are far better than nothing and
better than most small business boilerplate, because they were written for what
Ageless actually does rather than copied from a template for a different trade.

They were not written by a lawyer, and they were not reviewed by one.

## The thing that must be said plainly

No document can eliminate legal or regulatory risk. Anyone who tells you
otherwise is selling something. In particular, terms of service cannot:

- override the Ontario Consumer Protection Act, which applies whatever a
  contract says
- exclude liability for negligence causing death or personal injury
- override PIPEDA obligations about consent, access and breach reporting
- authorise anything that professional regulation prohibits
- bind somebody who never actually agreed to them

What good terms do is set expectations, define scope, allocate risk where the
law permits it, and give you something to point at in a dispute. That is
genuinely valuable. It is not immunity.

## Why the exposure here is above average

This business carries more risk than a typical local service company:

1. **Vulnerable clients.** Older adults, sometimes with cognitive change.
   Consent and capacity are live issues on every job.
2. **Safety-critical expectations.** Families will believe the equipment keeps
   their parent safe. When something goes wrong, the gap between what they
   believed and what was promised is where a claim lives.
3. **Health-adjacent information.** Falls history, medication timing, mobility.
   Sensitive under PIPEDA, with breach reporting duties attached.
4. **A protected title nearby.** A BScN is a degree. Nurse, RN and registered
   are protected titles in Ontario and appear nowhere on this site, deliberately.
5. **Third-party equipment.** Somebody else's device fails in your client's
   home. Who the client sues first is not always who made it.

## What to have a lawyer check, in priority order

1. **The liability limit in section 12 of the terms.** Limits of this kind are
   often read narrowly by Ontario courts, especially against consumers. Ask
   whether it holds and how to strengthen it.
2. **Whether the "not an emergency service" language is strong enough**, and
   whether it needs to be a signed acknowledgement at the assessment rather than
   a page on a website. This is the single largest exposure.
3. **Consent and capacity.** What to do when a family insists and the resident
   cannot meaningfully agree. Ask about a written consent form and what to
   accept as proof of a power of attorney.
4. **Cancellation and refund terms** against the Consumer Protection Act,
   including the rules on direct agreements signed in somebody's home, which
   carry specific cooling-off rights in Ontario.
5. **The privacy policy** against PIPEDA, particularly cross-border storage and
   breach reporting.
6. **Insurance.** Ask about commercial general liability and errors and
   omissions cover. Insurance, not contract wording, is what actually absorbs a
   claim. Nothing about insurance appears on the site because it has not been
   confirmed.

## Terms that were assumed, not given

These were written as sensible defaults. Confirm or change each one:

- 24 hours notice to cancel an assessment, full fee charged for a no-show
- 30 days notice to cancel the monthly service
- No refunds of part months
- Monthly service billed in advance
- Assessment fee payable at the time of the visit
- Client records kept for seven years after the service ends
- Non-client enquiries deleted within twelve months
