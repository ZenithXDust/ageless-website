Automatic pill dispensers: what to know before you buy

What these devices do well, where they fail, and the setup detail that decides whether one helps or just adds a beeping box to the kitchen.

What they actually do

An automatic dispenser holds a loaded tray of medication and releases only the dose that is due at the time it is due. The rest stays locked. Most will sound an alert when a dose is ready, and most can notify somebody else if a dose has not been taken after a set period.

That last feature is the real value. A weekly pill organiser tells you nothing until somebody physically inspects it. A connected dispenser turns a silent problem into a message on a phone, on the day it happens rather than a fortnight later.

Where they work well

  • Several medications at different times of day, which is where manual systems break down first.
  • Someone who is managing well but occasionally loses track, particularly with the evening dose.
  • Families at a distance who currently phone to check, and would rather not have that be the substance of every conversation.
  • Medication where doubling up is genuinely dangerous, since the locked compartment removes that possibility.

Where they fail

  • Anything not in the tray. Liquids, inhalers, injections, creams, and anything taken as needed rather than on a schedule. The dispenser gives a false sense that everything is covered when it is only covering the tablets.
  • Someone who does not want it. A person who is determined to work around the device will work around it. These are aids for people who want to get it right, not enforcement tools.
  • Refilling. Somebody has to load the tray correctly, usually weekly. If that person is the one already struggling to keep track, the dispenser has moved the problem rather than solved it. Work out who is going to do this before you buy.
  • Dexterity and eyesight. Some models need a firm press or a small tray removed. Try it, ideally with the person who will be using it.

The detail most people miss

The alert going to the wrong place. Out of the box, most of these devices notify one phone number, and it is frequently set to whoever unboxed it. If that person travels, works nights, or does not carry their phone, the missed dose alert goes nowhere.

Decide before installation who is notified, in what order, and what happens if the first person does not respond. Then test it by deliberately missing a dose and seeing what arrives. Almost nobody does this test, and it is the only way to know the thing works.

Before you buy anything

Get the current prescription list in front of you, including the as-needed items and anything that is not a tablet. Then work out what proportion of it a dispenser would actually cover. If it is most of it, this is likely worth doing. If half the routine is inhalers and eye drops, the dispenser solves less than it appears to, and the money may be better spent elsewhere.
