What to do when a parent leaves the stove on

It has happened once. Here is how to work out whether it matters, and what the options are between doing nothing and taking the stove away.

First, work out which kind of thing happened

Leaving a burner on is not one event, it is three different ones, and they need different responses.

  • Distraction. The phone rang. Everybody has done this. On its own it means very little.
  • A pattern. It has happened several times, or it is happening more often than it used to. That is worth acting on.
  • Not recognising it afterwards. Being shown the burner and having no memory of it, or not understanding why it matters. That is a different conversation, and one for a doctor rather than for a technology company.

Before deciding anything, find out which of the three you are looking at. Families frequently jump from the first to a decision that would only be justified by the third.

What not to do first

Do not remove the stove. It is the most common instinct and it is almost always too big a step for a first incident. Cooking is one of the last independent daily activities a person keeps, and taking it away has consequences that go well beyond dinner. It also breaks trust, which means the next incident gets hidden from you rather than reported.

Do not install a camera in the kitchen. It changes the relationship permanently and it does not stop a fire.

What is available between those two extremes

There is a lot of room between doing nothing and removing the appliance.

  • A stove shutoff device. These cut power to the hob after a set time with no motion in the room. The cooking carries on as normal when somebody is standing there. This is the single most useful option for most homes.
  • Better smoke detection, placed properly. Many homes have alarms that cannot be heard from the bedroom, or that were silenced because they went off during normal cooking. A detector that nobody can hear is decoration.
  • Linked alerts. A smoke alarm that also sends a message to a family member turns a noise nobody hears into information somebody can act on.
  • A kettle with automatic shutoff, and a microwave. Unglamorous, cheap, and it removes a good share of the risk without removing any independence.

The mistake almost everybody makes

Setting the shutoff too aggressively. A device that cuts the power during a normal Sunday roast will be unplugged within a fortnight, and then there is no protection at all plus a family argument. The setting has to match how that person actually cooks. This is the part that gets skipped when equipment is bought online and fitted by somebody who has never watched the household make dinner.

How to raise it

Not as a verdict. "I noticed the burner was on, are you all right?" opens a conversation. "You left the stove on again" ends one. There is more on this in the article on having the safety conversation.
